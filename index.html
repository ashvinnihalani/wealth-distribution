FAQ

Where does the data come from?

Group totals come straight from the Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts, which report every quarter since 1989 the assets, debts and household counts of five wealth groups: the bottom 50%, the 50th–90th percentiles, the 90th–99th, the 99th–99.9th, and the top 0.1%. Every dollar figure on this site reconciles to those published totals.

The Fed only publishes five groups. How can the site show 100,000 buckets?

Slicing finer needs an assumption about how wealth is spread inside each group. Below the 99th percentile the shape comes from the Survey of Consumer Finances net-worth percentiles (2022 survey, as tabulated by DQYDJ). Above it, a Pareto curve is used. Splitting the top 0.1% into the top 0.01% and 0.001% uses the World Inequality Database's reported split, held constant over time, so the time trend for those two finest slices is the Fed's top-0.1% trend. This is the same approach as the Inequality in America companion document that inspired the site.

What does the "what counts as wealth" slider do?

It adds asset classes in order of how quickly they turn into spendable cash: deposits and money-market funds; then stocks, mutual funds and bonds; then retirement accounts, pensions, life insurance and annuities; then private business equity and other assets; then homes and consumer durables. "Subtract debts" takes off mortgages, consumer credit and other liabilities.

How are debts split up within a group?

Within each group, debts are spread so that assets minus debts follows the survey's net-worth shape, which is what keeps the poorest households underwater. The bottom few percent of households show negative net worth, as they do in the survey.

Why does a share sometimes show as undefined?

With only cash counted and debts subtracted, the country's households as a whole owe more than they hold, so a "share of the total" has no meaning. The charts then show dollars only.

Are the dollars inflation-adjusted?

The Fed's data is nominal. The inflation toggle uses CPI-U (FRED series CPIAUCSL) to restate every quarter in the latest quarter's dollars. Averages are per household.

Why do the sliders differ between pages?

Each page keeps its own settings, so you can compare, say, 5 buckets in 1989 on one page with 1,000 buckets today on another. The Composition page only needs a year, because it always shows every asset tier.
