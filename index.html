Wealth Distribution

The chart looks the same. The top does not.

Split America into five wealth groups and the picture has barely moved in a generation. Split it into a thousand, or a hundred thousand, and the richest bucket keeps pulling away. Then ask what that wealth actually is: cash in the bank, or a stock portfolio and a house.

How this is built

Group totals come straight from the Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts, which report every quarter since 1989 the assets, debts and household counts of five wealth groups: the bottom 50%, the 50th–90th percentiles, the 90th–99th, the 99th–99.9th, and the top 0.1%. Every dollar figure on this page reconciles to those published totals.

Buckets. The Fed only publishes five groups, so slicing finer needs an assumption about how wealth is spread inside each group. Below the 99th percentile the shape comes from the Survey of Consumer Finances net-worth percentiles (2022 survey, as tabulated by DQYDJ). Above it, a Pareto curve is used. Splitting the top 0.1% into the top 0.01% and 0.001% uses the World Inequality Database's reported split, held constant over time, so the time trend for those two finest slices is the Fed's top-0.1% trend. This is the same approach as the Inequality in America companion document that inspired the page.

Liquidity. The slider adds asset classes in order of how quickly they turn into spendable cash: deposits and money-market funds; then stocks, mutual funds and bonds; then retirement accounts, pensions, life insurance and annuities; then private business equity and other assets; then homes and consumer durables. "Subtract debts" takes off mortgages, consumer credit and other liabilities. Within each group, debts are spread so that assets minus debts follows the survey's net-worth shape, which is what keeps the poorest households underwater. Note that with only cash counted, the country's households as a whole owe more than they hold, so wealth shares are undefined and the chart shows dollars only.

Dollars. Nominal by default in the Fed's data; the inflation toggle uses CPI-U (FRED series CPIAUCSL) to restate every quarter in the latest quarter's dollars. Averages are per household. "Screens of cash" uses the video's scale: one bill is $50,000, a screen-high stack is 100 bills, or $5 million.
